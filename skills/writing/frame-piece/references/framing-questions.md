# Framing Questions — the bank, and why each one works

Last updated: 2026-09-01

Used in `frame-piece` Step 3, and by `llm-wiki-book` for book-scale theses. Ask in the author's own language. Never ask all of these in one round — pick the two or three that the current candidates most need answered.

## The four load-bearing questions

These extract what the corpus cannot contain. Everything else on this page is secondary.

### 1. 「为什么是现在？同样的问题三年前问，答案会不一样吗？」

*Why now? Would the same question have a different answer three years ago?*

**Ask this first.** It is the question that manufactures the 议题, and it is the one most often mistaken for configuration. Material is usually timeless — a course on how memory works was as true in 2014 as today. Questions are not. What makes a settled topic contestable again is a change *outside* the material: a new technology, a new working condition, a practice the field adopted since, a cost that used to be free.

Without an occasion, the piece has nothing to argue about and opens with a definition. With one, the material stops being the subject and becomes evidence — which is the difference between a piece and a book review.

**Follow-ups:**
- "What can the reader do today that they couldn't when this material was made?"
- "Who is currently claiming the old advice is obsolete? Are they right?"
- "Has the cost of following this advice gone up or down?"

**Failure signal:** "Nothing has really changed." Sometimes true — and then the honest move is to say the material doesn't support a *timely* piece, and either find a different occasion or write an explainer and call it one.

### 2. 「读这些材料时，哪一句让你想反驳？」

*Reading this material, which sentence made you want to argue back?*

A disagreement sits at the intersection of material and author — which is exactly where a side lives. A flat restatement is impossible to derive from a disagreement, so any answer here immediately rules out that failure mode.

**Push the answer outward.** "The course contradicts itself" is a book review. "The course's advice fails under condition X" is a piece. Take whatever the author objects to and ask what *external* condition makes the objection bite — that condition is the occasion, and the objection is the 反方.

**Follow-ups when the answer is thin:**
- "Which part is overstated rather than wrong?"
- "Where does this advice work in a classroom and fail in your actual work?"
- "What would someone who has done this for ten years find naïve here?"

**Failure signal:** "It all seems reasonable." That usually means the author read the material as a student, not as a practitioner. Re-ask against a specific section, quoting it.

### 3. 「这个领域里大部分人相信什么，而你不相信？」

*What does most of this field believe that you don't?*

Establishes 正方 — the position the piece argues against. A piece with no opponent is a summary with better paragraphs. It also sets the reader's starting position: the piece's job is to move them off it.

**State 正方 at full strength.** The believers must be able to sign it. A 正方 that reads as obviously wrong is a strawman, and a piece that beats a strawman has no stakes — the reader was never on that side to begin with.

**Follow-ups:**
- "Who taught them to believe it?" (the opponent is more persuasive when named)
- "Is the common belief wrong, or just applied where it doesn't hold?" (the second is usually the sharper piece)

**Failure signal:** an answer that no one actually holds. "Most people think learning doesn't matter" is a strawman. Test by naming a specific person, book, or community that holds the belief; if none exists, discard it.

### 4. 「你自己在这件事上判断错过什么？」

*What did you personally get wrong about this?*

Earned authority. The mistake the author actually made is the one example no source can supply and no competitor can copy. It also converts an abstract position into a story with a cost attached, which is what makes a reader trust it.

**Follow-ups:**
- "How long did you believe the wrong thing, and what did it cost?"
- "What made you change your mind — evidence, or an incident?"

**Failure signal:** a generic mistake ("I used to not study enough"). Push for a dated, specific incident. If there genuinely isn't one, the author may be writing outside their experience — note it, and expect the piece to lean on borrowed examples.

## Ordinary framing questions

Configuration, not judgment. Ask quickly, last, and skip any the materials already answer.

| Question | Why it changes the piece |
| :--- | :--- |
| Who is reading, and what do they already believe? | Sets the distance the argument has to travel |
| Technical or business register? | Decides example type, jargon policy, and diagram density |
| What should change in their behaviour after? | If nothing, the payoff section has nothing to carry |
| Length and venue? | Bounds the selection map — a 1,200-word post cuts more than a chapter |

## Questions that don't work — and what to ask instead

| Don't ask | Why it fails | Ask instead |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "What's your angle?" | Requires the author to have already done the work they came for | Propose candidates, ask which one is wrong |
| "What's the main theme of these materials?" | Answered by the corpus, so the answer is the corpus's thesis | "Which claim here would you not repeat to a friend?" |
| "Do you like this angle?" | Invites approval, not judgment | "What does this angle force you to leave out?" |
| "Should I write about X or Y?" | Makes the skill's job the author's job | Rank them yourself, with a reason, and ask what the ranking gets wrong |
| "What's wrong with this material?" | Produces a book review — the source becomes the opponent, and the piece has no reader outside people who read it | "Where does this advice break down in 2026 that it didn't before?" |
| "Is this material's argument sound?" | Same trap, politer. Soundness is a reviewer's question, not an author's | "Who is currently arguing the opposite, and what do they have that this doesn't?" |

## Reading the answers

- **A strong answer is specific and slightly uncomfortable.** Authors hedge on positions they actually hold; fluent agreement usually means they haven't got one yet.
- **Keep the exact wording.** The author's own phrasing goes into the brief's *Author's markers* verbatim — that vocabulary is what makes the finished piece sound like a person. Paraphrasing at this stage discards the asset.
- **A refusal is data.** "I don't have a position on that" narrows the field honestly; record it as out of scope rather than pushing until an opinion is manufactured.
